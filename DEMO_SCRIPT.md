## Likely questions and answers

**Q1. Why circuit-level noise instead of a simpler model?**
Real hardware has faulty gates and faulty measurements, not just random flips on data qubits. Circuit-level noise includes all of that, so the decoder also has to handle measurement errors across rounds. It is the standard, more realistic benchmark.

**Q2. What is a threshold?**
It is the physical error rate below which making the code bigger makes the logical qubit better. For PyMatching we estimate the d=3 / d=5 crossing at about 0.0063, so roughly 0.63 percent. At p = 0.002, going from d=3 to d=5 cuts PyMatching's logical error rate by a factor of 2.6.

**Q3. Why does PyMatching win (or lose) at d=5?**
At d=5 the syndrome has 120 detectors and many more error patterns; matching handles this exactly through the graph structure, while a small network must learn it from examples. At p = 0.005 the MLP is 2.08 times and the CNN 1.40 times PyMatching's error rate (at d=3: 0.95 and 0.91).

**Q4. Could the network handle more rounds than it was trained on?**
Not as built. Both networks have a fixed input size (24 or 120 detectors, d rounds). A different number of rounds changes the input shape. A convolution over time or a recurrent/transformer model could handle that; PyMatching handles any number of rounds automatically.

**Q5. Why train on a mix of noise levels?**
At low p almost all syndromes are empty and a network can collapse to always answering 'no flip'. Mixing in higher p gives it informative examples, and one model then covers the whole sweep. For comparison, an MLP trained only at p = 0.005 has 1.01 times the error rate of the mixed-p MLP at p = 0.002 and 1.02 times at p = 0.012, so at d=3 single-p training happened to transfer well too.

**Q6. What is a Wilson interval?**
A 95% confidence interval for a success/failure rate. Unlike the simple plus-or-minus formula it never goes below zero and it still gives a sensible upper bound when we see zero errors. When two decoders' intervals overlap we call them statistically tied.

**Q7. Why does decoder speed matter?**
A superconducting quantum computer produces a new round of syndrome data about every microsecond. If decoding is slower than that, a backlog builds up. On our laptop at d=5 the single-shot latency is 10.3 µs for PyMatching, 204.4 µs for the MLP and 545.5 µs for the CNN.

**Q8. What would a real-time decoder need?**
Microsecond-scale latency with streaming input, so dedicated hardware such as FPGAs or ASICs, compact models (the base paper points to aggressive INT4 quantization for neural decoders), and decoding in sliding windows rather than whole blocks.

**Q9. Does PyMatching need to know the noise level?**
It uses the noise model to set edge weights. Using weights for p = 0.005 at p = 0.012 changes its d=5 error rate by a factor of 0.983, so matching is quite insensitive to this mismatch.

**Q10. How big are the networks?**
MLP d=3: 11,521 parameters, CNN d=3: 187,937 parameters, MLP d=5: 72,193 parameters, CNN d=5: 352,353 parameters. PyMatching has no trained parameters; its graph comes from the circuit.
